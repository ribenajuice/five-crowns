Five Crowns Ledger

The paper stays. You photograph the finished sheet, it becomes rows in a database, and years of games turn into something you can ask questions of.

This is the plan, not the thing. Nothing is built and nothing should be until you have read this and said which half is wrong.

The sheet proves itself

Reading handwriting is unreliable. A 7 becomes a 1, a 4 becomes a 9, and a photograph taken at an angle in kitchen light makes it worse. Normally that would make this a coin toss.

But a Five Crowns sheet has a property most documents don't: eleven round scores per player, and a total at the bottom. The rounds must add up to the total.

So every column carries its own proof. If the transcription doesn't add up, it is wrong — and the app knows that before you do.

rounds 3–134 · 0 · 12 · 7 · 3 · 15 · 0 · 22 · 8 · 30 · 11
reads as112
written total112
✓ adds up — saved without asking you
rounds 3–134 · 0 · 12 · 1 · 3 · 15 · 0 · 22 · 8 · 30 · 11
reads as106
written total112
✗ six short — one number in this column is misread, and you are shown which

That is the difference between a tool you trust and a database quietly full of wrong numbers. Analytics built on unverified transcription is worse than no analytics — it is confidently wrong, and you would never know.

What it does

One loop, after the game, from wherever you are.

  1. Photograph the finished sheet

    From a phone, at the table, before the pad goes back in the drawer. One photo, whatever the lighting.

  2. It reads the grid

    Five Crowns has a known shape — players across the top, eleven rounds down, totals at the bottom. Knowing the shape in advance is most of what makes reading it possible.

  3. It shows you what it read, beside the photo

    Every column that adds up is marked as sound. Every column that doesn't is flagged, with the round it is least sure about highlighted.

  4. You correct what's wrong

    Type over the numbers it got wrong. Usually none, sometimes one. Nothing is saved until the arithmetic agrees.

  5. It becomes a game in the log

    Date, players, every round, the winner. The photo is kept with it, so any number can be checked against the paper for as long as the record exists.

Keeping the photo is not sentiment. It is what lets you re-read a game in five years when the analytics say something surprising and you want to know whether the data or the conclusion is wrong.

What it deliberately is not

Each of these is a decision, not an omission.

  • Not a scoreboard. Nobody opens it during a game. The paper is the game; this is the archive.
  • It does not know the rules. No wild cards, no going out, no scoring a hand. It reads what you wrote and adds it up. Every rule it learns is a rule that can disagree with the pad in your hand — and the pad wins.
  • No accounts, no other players logging in. One keeper of the record, which is how the pad works now.
  • Analytics are not in the first version — but every decision here is made so they are possible later. See below.

The analytics come later, and the plan is shaped for them now

You said analytics eventually. That doesn't mean building them now — it means not storing the data in a way that forecloses them.

Storing a game as one row with a final score would be the mistake. Storing every round separately costs nothing extra today and is what later makes these answerable:

  • Who actually wins, over years rather than over a night
  • Whether the late rounds — the big hands — are where games are really decided
  • Whether anyone is genuinely consistent, or just occasionally lucky
  • What a typical round score looks like as the hands grow from three cards to thirteen

None of that needs building now. All of it needs the rounds kept.

The risk, named plainly

Handwriting recognition is the whole risk, and it will get things wrong. The arithmetic check catches errors that change a column's total — which is most of them. It cannot catch two errors that cancel out (a 7 read as 1 and a 3 read as 9 in the same column), and it cannot catch a total you wrote down wrong yourself.

That is why you see what it read, next to the photo, every time — even when it adds up. The app's job is to be checkable, not to be right.

What I need from you

These change the shape of the first version, so they are worth answering before anything is built.

Is there a stack of old sheets to catch up on? If there are years of pads in a drawer, the first version is a bulk import — and that is a different app from one that takes a photo a week. If it starts from tonight, it is much simpler.
Does the same group play, or does it vary? A fixed four or five means players are just names. A changing group means a player list, and "is this the same Sam as last year" becomes a real question the moment you run analytics.
What does your sheet actually look like? A photo of one blank and one finished sheet is worth more than any description. It decides how hard the reading is — printed grid or hand-ruled, running totals or just a final, names at the top or down the side.
Phone only, or the PC too? The photo is taken on a phone. Whether you also want to sit at the PC and go through a backlog changes what gets built first.

What happens next

  • First thing built is the reading step, alone, tested against your real sheets. If a photograph of your actual pad cannot be read reliably, that is worth knowing in an afternoon rather than after a website exists around it.
