Five Crowns Ledger — three visual directions

Three different-looking apps, and — more importantly — three different answers to the review screen: how you compare a photo of the pad against the numbers we read off it, on a phone, at a kitchen table, in bad light.

Every screen below is a real mockup at phone width. The names are yours and the numbers are off the two sheets you sent. Tap through all three, then jump to “What I need you to pick” at the end.

Direction 1 — Kitchen Table

The app looks like the pad. Warm paper, biro-blue ink, a soft serif for the headings and big tabular numbers. It is quiet, it is legible in bad light, and it never competes with the photo for attention. The personality lives in the words, not the decoration.

Its answer to the review screen: the Column Sweep. You never compare a whole grid against a whole photo. You check one player's column at a time, with that column of the photo cropped out and stood right next to the numbers, row for row. Four or five short comparisons instead of one impossible one.

The front door, and waiting

The review screen — Column Sweep

The problem: a wrong digit that still climbs is invisible to the app. Only you can catch it, on a phone, against a photo of handwriting. The answer here: crop the photo down to one player's column, put it beside that player's numbers with the rows lined up, and let you sweep down eleven pairs. The eye travels about a centimetre, and the row you are on is never in doubt.

Direction 2 — The Card Room

Dark green baize, brass and ivory. A members' room rather than a kitchen: the password screen is a door, the records board is an honours board screwed to the wall, and winning gets engraved. Warm, but grown-up-warm — candlelight rather than crayons. It is also the easiest of the three to read at night on the sofa, and the hardest to read in bright sun.

Its answer to the review screen: the Loupe. The whole sheet stays pinned at the top of the screen, all the time. The full grid of numbers sits underneath. Tapping any number drives the photo — it zooms and pans to that exact cell and rings it. You keep the whole page in view and let the app do the finger-work of finding the line.

The review screen — the Loupe

The bet this direction makes: that you'd rather see the whole page and have the app find the line for you, than have the page chopped into columns. It needs no clever cropping of the photo, and it is the only one of the three where you can see two columns of handwriting at once. The cost is that the numbers are smaller and the eye has further to travel.

The honours board

Direction 3 — Games Night

Loud, chunky and funny. Deep plum and tangerine, fat rounded shapes, hard offset shadows, a heavy display face. Records are stickers you'd want to win. This is the direction that most obviously isn't a business dashboard — “the player getting absolutely wrecked” looks at home here in a way it doesn't in the other two.

Its answer to the review screen: Overlay & Blink. There is no side-by-side at all. What we read is printed on top of the photo, exactly where the number is, and a three-way switch flicks between Paper, Both and Ours. Anything we got wrong jumps out as a change when you flick — the same trick astronomers use to spot a moving dot between two photographs.

The review screen — Overlay & Blink

The bet this direction makes: that the fastest way to compare two things is to put them in the same place and switch between them, rather than to look from one to the other. It's the boldest of the three and the riskiest — it depends on us knowing precisely where each number sits on the paper, and it gets crowded with five columns on a small screen.   1. Which look?

    Kitchen Table (paper & biro) · The Card Room (baize & brass) · Games Night (plum & tangerine).

    My recommendation: Kitchen Tablerecommended — it's the one that stays legible in the conditions you actually use it in, and it lets the photo be the loudest thing on the review screen. Games Night is the most fun and I'd happily build it; my only worry is that the review screen is a serious screen and this palette shouts on it. The Card Room is the best-looking at night and the worst in a bright kitchen.

  2. Which review mechanic?

    This is the real decision. Everything else is paint.

    My recommendation: Column Sweep as the main path, with the whole sheet always one tap awayrecommended — it's the only one that guarantees you're looking at the right line, it turns one impossible comparison into four or five short ones, and it flows straight into the close-up re-photograph, which is the single biggest accuracy lever we have. If you'd rather see the whole page, say so and I'll make the Loupe primary instead.

  3. Should marking a column “checked” be required before saving?

    The sweep shows a tick per column. It could be a progress aid, or a gate.

    My recommendation: an aid, not a gate. A forced tick teaches you to tap four times without looking, and then the tick means nothing. Save stays blocked only on the hard checks — eleven numbers per column, always climbing.

  4. Before 10 games, do you want an “unofficial” table?

    The board is sealed until 10 games. I could show a scoreboard marked “doesn't count yet” underneath the countdown.

    My recommendation: no. An unofficial table is the thing everyone screenshots, and then the sealing was pointless. The countdown plus the games list is enough. Easy to add later if the wait annoys you.

  5. Should the app hint at hands that “look odd”?

    We can flag an unusually big single hand as a soft, non-blocking warning. But one of your real sheets has a genuine 51-point hand that any such rule would flag wrongly.

    My recommendation: no warning, but keep the hand column prominent. A hint that cries wolf on real numbers trains you to ignore it — and that same instinct is what makes the review step work at all.

  6. The voice.

    “Put it in the book” · “Two things to sort” · “Nothing gets crowned on four games” · “The drought”. Warm, dry, a bit blunt, no exclamation marks.

    Tell me if it's too matey or not matey enough. The admin panel stays completely plain either way — no jokes near the API key.

  7. One display font, or system only?

    Each direction uses one personality face for headings and numbers (Fraunces / Playfair Display / Baloo 2) over the phone's own font for body text.

    My recommendation: keep the one face. It's most of what makes each direction feel like something rather than a form. It costs one small font file.

  8. Dark mode: follow the phone, or force one?

    My recommendation: follow the phone. You'll be using it in a dim kitchen and on a sofa at midnight. Both modes are drawn and both pass contrast.
